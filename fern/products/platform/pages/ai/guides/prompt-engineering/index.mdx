A prompt is the instruction set an AI agent works from: who it is, what it's there to accomplish,
and how to handle the conversation in front of it. You write it in plain language, and the model
reads it literally.

An agent takes prompts in more than one place. The main prompt carries its identity and its task,
while [context steps][context-steps], [SignalWire AI Gateway (SWAIG) functions][swaig], the
[conscience][conscience], and the [post-prompt][post-prompt] each accept prompts of their own.
[Prompt surfaces][prompt-surfaces] covers all five.

Think of prompt engineering like giving detailed instructions to a new team member: for them to succeed,
you need to be clear about what you want them to do, how to do it, and what counts as done. 
A good prompt tells the AI how to handle user questions, what tone to use, and what information to focus on.
The rules that have to hold on every call belong in your code, where they are enforced instead of requested.

## What makes a prompt work

How you write your prompts determines your AI's performance: a well-prompted agent stays
consistent from caller to caller, handles real back-and-forth conversation, and recovers
gracefully when things get confusing.
The best prompts share a few traits.
They use clear, specific language, because the AI takes instructions literally.
They organize information with headings and consistent formatting, so the AI can tell how
the pieces fit together.
They leave room for real conversations, which rarely follow a script.
And they stay lean.

### Avoid overprompting

A model arrives knowing grammar, phone manners, and how to structure a sentence, so lines spent on
any of that dilute the instructions only you can supply. Those are your domain's vocabulary, the
constraints of your use case, and the shape an answer has to arrive in.

Business data is where overprompting does the most damage. A prompt carrying the whole rate card,
the full menu, and every policy tends to survive the demo and break on real callers. The moment a
figure changes, the prompt is wrong, and a model asked to multiply a mileage by a rate sometimes gets
the arithmetic wrong even when the rate is right. That data belongs behind a
[tool call][tool-calling]. What stays in the prompt is the part no lookup would answer
differently: the vehicle types, the accessible option, the lost-property extension.

## Building your prompt structure

A prompt reads better when each part of it answers a different question the model would otherwise
guess at. The dispatcher prompt below is organized under six headings, described here in the order
they appear. Treat them as a starting shape rather than a fixed schema: a narrow agent may need only
the first and the last.

### Role

Establish who the agent is. This identity sets the tone and the expertise level for everything that
follows, and it is usually the shortest section in the prompt. "You are Ada, the dispatcher for
Bayview Taxi" gives the model a persona to hold for the whole conversation.

### Knowledge base

The background the agent needs to make sense of what callers say: your vocabulary, the options you
offer, and what a complete request looks like. Bayview's dispatcher has to know that vehicles come in
three types and that a booking needs a pickup, a destination, and a time. Keep this section to facts
that don't change, and put anything that moves behind a [tool call][tool-calling].

### Tools

Name each function the agent can call and say when to call it. The function's own description carries
the interface, so what belongs here is the sequencing, which is the part models get wrong. "Call
get_quote once you have both addresses, and every time either one changes" has nowhere else to live.

### Task structure

The order a conversation runs in, written as steps. Without it the agent improvises the sequence and
will eventually price a trip before it has confirmed the addresses. Write the path you expect and let
the guardrails cover the rest.

### Response guidelines

How the agent sounds and what shape its answers take. Choosing between "friendly, simple language
with clear steps" and "professional but approachable, getting straight to the point" keeps the
conversation aligned with your brand voice. Rules about reading values back exactly as a function
returned them belong here too.

### Guardrails

Guardrails are the rules that keep the agent in bounds: what it won't promise, what it won't decide on
its own, what it hands off. Most of them work better outside the prompt than in it, so the prompt
carries only the few that can't live anywhere else.
[Where guardrails belong](#where-guardrails-belong) covers the order to reach for them in.

### Example structure

Here are all six sections in one prompt, for Bayview Taxi's dispatcher, the agent the rest of these
guides build on:

```markdown
## Role
You are Ada, the dispatcher for Bayview Taxi. You quote fares and book pickups.

## Knowledge base
- Vehicle types: standard, van, wheelchair accessible
- Payment: card over the phone, or cash to the driver
- A booking needs a pickup address, a destination, and a pickup time

## Tools
- get_quote: prices a trip and returns the fare and the distance. Call it once you have both addresses, and every time either one changes.
- book_ride: dispatches a driver and returns the pickup time and vehicle. Call it only after the caller has accepted a quote.

## Task structure
1. Greet the caller and ask where they are and where they're going
2. To quote a trip:
   - Collect the pickup address and the destination
   - Confirm both back to the caller before pricing
   - Call get_quote and read the fare it returns
3. To book:
   - Ask which vehicle type they need
   - Call book_ride, then read back the pickup time and vehicle

## Response guidelines
- Use a calm, efficient tone; callers are often in a hurry
- Confirm addresses back before acting on them
- Read fares and pickup times exactly as the functions return them
- Offer the nearest alternative when a caller asks for something unavailable

## Guardrails
- Fares, surcharges, and distances come from get_quote; relay its answer rather than working them out
- Availability and pickup times are decided by book_ride
- If asked how long a trip will take, give the estimate book_ride returned and say traffic can change it
```

<Info>
The prompt holds the shape of a booking — the vehicle types, the ways to pay, what a booking needs —
and nothing whose answer can change. Fares move with distance and time of day, so they come from
`get_quote`; pickup times depend on which driver is free, so they come from `book_ride`. Both reach
your backend through a [tool call][tool-calling].
</Info>

## Where guardrails belong

A prompt full of prohibitions is one of the most common ways an agent becomes unpredictable.
Every rule you add competes with the others for the model's attention, and a prohibition is followed
less reliably than the same rule written as an instruction.
Work through the options below in order, and stop as soon as the behavior is covered.

1. **State what success looks like.** An agent that knows its objective works out most of its
   own limits. "Quote the trip, book it once the caller accepts, and hand off anything you
   can't complete" already rules out competitor talk and freelance discounts, without a
   single prohibition.
2. **Enforce hard rules in code.** Prices, discounts, eligibility, and anything that must be
   exact or current belong behind a [tool call][tool-calling], where your
   handler validates the caller's input and decides the outcome. Your handler enforces a pricing
   rule on every call; the prompt only asks.
3. **Remove the possibility.** Scope which functions exist at each stage with
   [context steps][context-steps]: the agent can't book before it quotes if the booking
   function isn't available yet.
4. **Put global rules in the conscience.** Tone, persona, safety, and compliance rules that
   apply to the whole call belong in the [conscience][conscience]. It is reinforced throughout
   the conversation, including after function calls, so the rule doesn't fade the way one line
   in the middle of a long main prompt does.
5. **Then, sparingly, write a don't.** A short list of genuine non-negotiables that can't be
   enforced structurally still earns its place. Keep it specific, and phrase it as the
   behavior you want wherever you can: "If asked how long the trip will take, give the estimate
   book_ride returned and say traffic can change it" steers better than "Don't promise arrival times".

## Iterative refinement process

A first prompt is a draft. A production prompt is grown: you test it against real conversations,
watch where the agent hesitates or drifts, and change one thing at a time.

Start from clear objectives — the agent's purpose, its scope, and what counts as a finished call —
and from what you know about the people who will call. Their vocabulary, how much of your
terminology they share, and the problems that put them on the phone all shape what the prompt has
to cover.

Then build in layers. Begin with a prompt that handles the most common call, expand it as unusual
cases surface, and trim anything that doesn't change behavior. Trimming is a concrete step: delete
one line, run your test calls again, and keep the deletion if the agent behaves the same without it.
A line that changes nothing was competing for the model's attention and buying nothing back. The
usual candidates are instructions restating something the model already does well, and rules that
duplicate something a function already enforces.

Test each revision the same way every time. Functional testing comes first: run the standard requests
and confirm the responses still hold. Adversarial testing comes next — try to talk the agent out of
its rules, and check that the ones surviving are the ones your code enforces. Boundary testing pushes
at the edges of what the agent knows, and where your traffic supports it, A/B testing compares two
prompt versions against real callers. Keep the prompt under version control alongside its test cases,
plus a note of what each revision changed. A regression then points back at the line that caused it.

### Four rounds on the dispatcher prompt

Each round below starts from something the previous prompt got wrong on a real call. Two things
change as it goes: the conversation gets more specific, and every fact with a right answer moves out
of the prose and behind a function.

<Tabs>
<Tab title="Draft">

One line. It gets a demo working and tells you nothing about what the agent will do on call fifty.

```markdown
Take taxi bookings over the phone.
```

</Tab>
<Tab title="After the calls wandered">

Test calls ended without a destination, or with the caller waiting on an agent that had moved on.
The prompt gets a shape for the conversation to follow.

```markdown
You are Ada, the dispatcher for Bayview Taxi.

Steps:
1. Greet the caller and identify yourself
2. Ask where they are
3. Ask where they are going
4. Tell them what happens next
```

</Tab>
<Tab title="After a made-up fare">

Asked what a ride would cost, the agent said "about eighteen dollars" — a figure nothing in the
system had produced. The facts move behind functions, and the prompt says when to call each one.

```markdown
You are Ada, the dispatcher for Bayview Taxi.

Tools:
- get_quote: prices a trip and returns the fare and distance. Call it once you have both addresses.
- book_ride: dispatches a driver and returns the pickup time and vehicle.

Steps:
1. Greet the caller and identify yourself
2. Collect the pickup address and the destination
3. Confirm both back to the caller, then call get_quote
4. Read back only the fare get_quote returned
5. If the caller accepts, call book_ride and read back the pickup time
6. Confirm the caller has what they need before ending
```

</Tab>
<Tab title="After callers went off script">

Callers changed the destination mid-quote, rang back to ask where their car was, and wanted a person
for a fare dispute. The last round adds a function for each of those outcomes, plus one escape hatch
for everything the agent shouldn't attempt.

```markdown
You are Ada, the dispatcher for Bayview Taxi. Stay calm and efficient, and keep replies to a
couple of sentences so the caller can interrupt.

Tools:
- get_quote: prices a trip and returns the fare and distance.
- book_ride: dispatches a driver and returns the pickup time and vehicle.
- check_ride_status: returns where a booked driver is.
- transfer_to_dispatcher: hands the call to a human dispatcher.

Steps:
1. Greet the caller and identify yourself.
2. Collect the pickup address and the destination. Spell back unusual street names to confirm them.
3. Call get_quote and read back only the fare it returned. Never work a fare out yourself.
4. If either address changes, call get_quote again before quoting anything.
5. If the caller accepts, call book_ride and read back the pickup time and vehicle.
6. If the caller asks where their car is, call check_ride_status every time rather than repeating
   an earlier answer.
7. Close by confirming the caller has what they need.

Call transfer_to_dispatcher for fare disputes, lost property, anything outside booking a ride, or
after any function fails twice.
```

</Tab>
</Tabs>

The rules a prompt like this usually accumulates — never quote a fare below the minimum, never
promise a car that isn't free, always log the booking — are absent from the final version on purpose.
`get_quote` decides the fare, `book_ride` decides whether a driver exists, and both log what they did.
